How tidemark works

No science background required.

tidemark reads up to 45 years of NASA data for any place on Earth and answers two questions that no other tool answers: when will a climate trend become statistically confirmed, and what will it reach after that.

What is a trend?

A trend is a consistent direction of change over time. If your city's average temperature was slightly higher each decade for 40 years in a row, that is a trend. If it went up some years and down others with no overall direction, that is noise.

The tricky part is telling the difference. Any short stretch of years can look like a trend just by chance. Scientists use a test called Mann-Kendall to check whether the direction is consistent enough to be real, or whether it could just be a lucky run of similar years.

tidemark runs that test on real NASA data for whatever location and variable you pick, then goes further and tells you what the result means in plain terms.

What does "statistically significant" mean?

A result is called statistically significant when the chance of seeing it in purely random data is less than 5 in 100. Scientists write this as p < 0.05.

Think of it like a court verdict. p < 0.05 is not proof that the trend is real. It means the evidence is strong enough that we would be wrong to ignore it. p > 0.05 means the data is not yet convincing, not that nothing is happening.

The p-value depends partly on how many years of data you have. A short record cannot confirm a small trend even if the trend is real. This is the central insight that tidemark is built around.

An analogy

Imagine you flip a coin and get heads seven times in a row. That feels suspicious, but it happens in roughly 1 in 128 fair flips. With only seven flips you cannot prove the coin is rigged. With 1000 flips and 700 heads, the evidence is overwhelming.

Climate data works the same way. A small warming trend needs a long record before the signal rises above the background noise. tidemark tells you how long that record needs to be.

The question no tool was answering

Dr. James Aker at NASA Goddard said it plainly in the challenge brief: "Sometimes all a trend needs to become significant is a longer period." Every existing tool ignores that sentence. tidemark is built around it.

Every existing climate analysis tool, from NASA Giovanni to the R trend package, runs the significance test and returns two numbers: the slope of the trend, and whether p is below 0.05. Then it stops.

If the answer is "not yet significant", you are left with nothing. You do not know if you need five more years of data or fifty. You do not know whether the trend is real but underseen, or genuinely absent. No tool told you.

Cairo rainfall is a concrete example of what that silence costs. The standard analytical formula, which every existing tool uses, says this trend will become significant around 2128. tidemark's Monte Carlo estimate says 2051. The 77-year gap exists because Cairo's rainfall residuals are right-skewed, and the standard formula assumes a bell curve. tidemark detects that mismatch, names it, and directs you to the estimate that is correct for the actual noise structure.

What tidemark adds

If a trend is not yet significant, tidemark projects forward in time to find the year when the data record would be long enough for the test to pass. It calls this the significance trajectory.

If a trend is already significant, tidemark projects the slope forward to named real-world thresholds: the Paris Agreement temperature targets, the World Meteorological Organization drought classifications, and the FAO crop water stress limits. It returns a year, not a range, not a scenario.

How the prediction works

The Mann-Kendall test produces a score called Z. When Z passes 1.96, the trend is considered significant. The key fact is that Z grows predictably as you add more years of data. The math behind this means the crossing year is calculable.

tidemark computes that crossing year four independent ways and compares them. Each method makes different assumptions about the shape of the data. If all four agree, the estimate is trustworthy. If they disagree, tidemark diagnoses why and tells you which one to trust.

Why four methods?

Two of the methods assume that the random variation in the data follows a bell curve, the way heights in a population do. That assumption is fine for temperature. It is wrong for rainfall in dry regions, where most months are near zero and occasional heavy rains spike far above the average.

When you apply the bell-curve method to skewed data, you get a confident wrong answer. For Cairo's rainfall, the two formula-based methods predict significance around 2128. The simulation method, which makes no assumption about the data shape, says 2051. That is a 77-year error.

tidemark detects this automatically. It runs a normality test on the residuals, checks whether the methods diverge, and if they do, it shows you which methods are invalid and routes you to the correct one.

What is Monte Carlo?

Monte Carlo is a way of using random simulation to answer a question you cannot solve with a formula. The name comes from the casino in Monaco, because the method is built on random draws.

tidemark runs 1000 simulations. In each one, it extends the existing data record forward by drawing randomly from the actual residuals, the year-to-year wobble already in the real data. It then checks when the significance test passes in each simulation. The median year across all 1000 runs is the prediction. The spread of the results gives you the confidence interval.

Because it draws from the real data rather than from a theoretical bell curve, it handles skewed and irregular data correctly.

The theoretical minimum

There is a hard floor on how early any test can confirm a trend, set by the mathematics of information theory. It is called the Cramer-Rao bound. No method, however clever, can detect the trend in fewer years than this floor allows. It depends only on the noise level and the shape of the noise distribution.

For bell-curve noise, the Cramer-Rao bound agrees closely with the Weatherhead formula. For skewed noise, the bound is shorter, which means Weatherhead overstates how long detection will take. tidemark computes this bound on every result and shows it alongside the other three estimates. If the Monte Carlo crossing year is close to the bound, you are near the theoretical limit. If it is far above it, the noise structure is the obstacle.

The detection floor

Every result includes a detection floor: the smallest slope that the current record length could possibly confirm. If the actual trend is smaller than the floor, no test can find it yet, regardless of direction.

tidemark shows the ratio of your trend's slope to the floor. A ratio above 1.0 means the signal is large enough to detect. Below 1.0 means the record is simply too short to say anything yet, and the significance trajectory answer needs to be read in that light.

An analogy

It is like a scale that cannot read below 1 kilogram. If a package weighs 800 grams, the scale will not detect it, but the package still has weight. The floor tells you what the scale's minimum is so you know when to trust the reading and when to get a more sensitive instrument, which here means waiting for more years of data.

Separating climate from weather cycles

El Nino and La Nina are natural swings in Pacific Ocean temperatures that repeat every few years. They push global temperatures up or down by roughly half a degree and cause major swings in rainfall patterns across Asia, Africa, and the Americas. They are not a trend. They are a natural cycle.

If you run a trend test on raw temperature data without accounting for El Nino, you might detect a trend that is partly or entirely explained by which phase of the cycle the data happened to end on. tidemark removes the El Nino signal using NOAA's official MEI v2 index before running the test.

The output shows both results: the raw trend and the ENSO-corrected trend. If they are meaningfully different, it means the natural cycle was masking or amplifying the underlying long-term change.

Rank-based ENSO separation

tidemark also runs a partial Mann-Kendall test (Libiseller & Grimvall, 2002) that separates the trend signal from ENSO variability using a rank-based cross-covariance framework. Unlike the OLS ENSO correction, it requires no assumption about whether the ENSO-climate relationship is linear. It answers directly: is there a trend after ENSO variability is accounted for?

The output shows whether the ENSO-adjusted test changes the significance verdict, and what fraction of concordance variance ENSO explains. This is the only non-parametric ENSO separation test available in any live-data climate trend tool.

Correcting for skewed data

Precipitation, vegetation indices (NDVI), and evapotranspiration in dry regions follow right-skewed distributions: most years are near zero, with occasional large spikes. Standard variance corrections for the Mann-Kendall test, including Hamed-Rao, only account for autocorrelation. For right-skewed residuals, they systematically underestimate the variance of the test statistic, inflating the Z-score and producing false positives.

tidemark uses the MKC3 variance correction (Then et al., 2025) for precipitation, NDVI, and other variables with skewed residuals. MKC3 adds a third-order cumulant correction term that captures the skewness of lag products, which Hamed-Rao misses. For symmetric series the two corrections are mathematically identical. For Cairo precipitation (skewness ≈ 1.95), the correction changes the verdict from significant (Hamed-Rao, p ≈ 0.03) to not significant (MKC3, p > 0.10).

The correction is applied automatically when residual skewness exceeds 0.5. The test method is shown on every result so you always know which correction was used.

Where the data comes from

tidemark draws from four NASA data sources depending on the variable you select. All measurements are monthly averages or composites. No data is stored on our servers.

NASA POWER

Temperature, humidity, evaporation, wind, and soil variables come from NASA POWER (Prediction Of Worldwide Energy Resources), a free dataset produced at NASA Goddard combining MERRA-2 reanalysis with satellite observations. Coverage from 1981. No account required.

GPM IMERG

Precipitation comes from NASA GPM IMERG (Global Precipitation Measurement, Integrated Multi-satellitE Retrievals), accessed via NASA GES DISC. Coverage from 2001. Requires NASA Earthdata login.

AppEEARS MODIS

Land surface temperature and evapotranspiration come from MODIS satellite composites via NASA AppEEARS. Coverage from 2001. Requires NASA Earthdata login.

Variables available

  • Temperature (average, maximum, minimum) — 1981+
  • Precipitation — 2001+ (GPM IMERG)
  • Relative humidity — 1981+
  • Evaporation over land — 1981+
  • Wind speed — 1981+
  • Land surface temperature — 2001+ (MODIS)
  • Evapotranspiration — 2001+ (MODIS)
  • Vegetation index (NDVI) — 2000+ (MODIS)

Record length varies by variable. Temperature and humidity have up to 45 years (1981-2025). Precipitation, land surface temperature, and evapotranspiration start from 2001, giving up to 25 years.

What tidemark does not do

A significance trajectory is not a forecast. tidemark is not a climate model. It does not simulate future emissions, land use change, or ocean circulation. It assumes the current trend continues at its current rate. If conditions change, the trajectory changes.

The milestone projections for Paris Agreement temperatures and WMO drought thresholds are extrapolations of the existing trend, not predictions of what will happen. They answer the question: if nothing changes from the current trajectory, when would this variable reach this threshold?

Results are only as good as the underlying NASA data. POWER uses a 0.5 by 0.5 degree grid. For small islands, complex terrain, or coastal transition zones, the grid cell may not represent the precise location you have in mind.
