{# The gap: what no existing tool does #}


  

    

    
The gap we found

  

  

    

      
Dr. James Aker, NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, put the gap plainly in the
         challenge brief: "Sometimes all a trend needs to become significant is a longer
         period." No existing tool tells you how much longer. We built the one that does.

      
NASA Giovanni, NOAA Climate Explorer, Copernicus CDS Toolbox, NCL, the R trend
         package. Every one of them runs Mann-Kendall and returns two numbers: the slope, and
         whether p is below 0.05. That is where they stop.

      
Cairo rainfall shows what that gap costs. The standard analytical formula returns
         a crossing year around 2128. Tidemark's Monte Carlo estimate returns 2051. That 77-year
         difference is not a speed improvement. It is a correctness difference. Cairo's
         precipitation residuals are right-skewed. The analytical formula assumes a bell curve.
         Applied to skewed data, it gives a confident wrong answer. Tidemark runs a normality
         test on the residuals, detects the divergence, identifies the cause, and directs you
         to the method that is correct for the actual noise structure of your data.


      

      

        

          
What we derived

        

        

          
The Mann-Kendall Z-score grows proportional to n^(3/2) as the record
             lengthens. For a trend that is real, the year when Z crosses 1.96 is
             mathematically predictable. We derived that crossing year four independent
             ways and built a cross-validation layer that detects when they disagree and
             explains why.

          
This calculation exists in the statistical literature on early warning
             signals (Scheffer 2009, Lenton 2011) but has never been implemented as a
             working tool against live NASA satellite data. We wrote it from scratch,
             validated it against up to 45 years of NASA data across four sources, and
             exposed it through a CLI and a web interface.

        

      


      

      

        

          
Why four methods

        

        

          
The analytical formula assumes Gaussian noise. Tropical precipitation is not
             Gaussian. When you apply the wrong model, you get a confident wrong answer.
             Cairo rainfall: analytical formula says significance in 2128, Monte Carlo
             says 2051. A 77-year error, stated with false confidence.

          
We detect the divergence, run a normality test on the residuals, name the
             cause, and suppress the invalid estimates. The Monte Carlo method draws
             directly from the observed residual distribution and makes no normality
             assumption. When methods disagree, we route you to the one that is correct
             for the actual noise structure of your data.

        

      


      

      

        

          
The detection floor

        

        

          
No existing climate trend tool makes this explicit: there is a minimum slope
             below which the current record is too short to confirm anything. We compute
             it on every result, unconditionally, and show the ratio of the detected
             slope to the floor.

          
Ratio below 1.0 means the trend is unresolvable with the current record
             length, regardless of its direction. The significance trajectory and the
             detection floor together answer the question every analyst faces but no tool
             has answered: is the data telling me something, or is the record just too
             short to know?

        

      


      

      

        

          
The theoretical minimum

        

        

          
The Cramer-Rao bound sets the minimum number of years any statistical test
             could ever need to detect a trend, given the noise in the data. It is derived
             from Fisher information theory. No method can beat it. For Gaussian noise it
             agrees closely with the Weatherhead formula. For right-skewed noise it is
             shorter, meaning the standard formulas are pessimistic: the data could in
             principle confirm the trend sooner than they suggest.

          
Tidemark computes this bound on every result and shows it alongside the other
             three estimates. If the Monte Carlo estimate sits close to the CRB, you are
             near the theoretical limit. If it sits far above it, the data's noise
             structure is the obstacle, not a limitation of the test.

        

      


      

      

        

          
ENSO isolation

        

        

          
El Nino and La Nina add up to 0.5 degrees C of interannual noise to surface
             temperature and 20-40% swings to tropical precipitation. A raw Mann-Kendall
             on that signal mixes forced trend with natural oscillation. We regress out
             the NOAA MEI v2 index before running the test and report both the raw and
             corrected trajectory.

          
If the corrected slope differs meaningfully from the raw one, ENSO has been
             masking or amplifying the trend you are looking at. Knowing which is not a
             cosmetic improvement. It changes the crossing year.

        

      


      

      

        

          
After significance

        

        

          
Once a trend is confirmed, the next question is what it reaches and when.
             We project Sen's slope forward to named real-world thresholds: Paris
             Agreement +1.5C and +2.0C anomaly, WMO drought classifications, FAO crop
             water stress bounds. These are not invented. Each threshold comes from an
             established international index with a cited source.

          
The result is a year. Not a probability range, not a scenario. A year at the
             current rate. That is the number a policymaker or farmer can use.

All results below are from the actual pipeline against live NASA data. No demo data. Numbers shown for Dhaka temperature and Cairo precipitation used POWER (1981-2025). Dhaka rainfall and LST used GPM IMERG and AppEEARS MODIS (2001-2025).


      

      

        

          
Dhaka, Bangladesh
Temperature

        

        

          
Significant trend detected. Slope -0.021 degrees C per year over 45 years
             (p = 0.047). A statistically confirmed cooling signal, with a step change
             detected in 1999 (Pettitt p < 0.001). Floor ratio 1.6x, clearly above
             the detection threshold. Seasonal breakdown shows the
             strongest signal in winter (DJF, p < 0.001).

Cairo, Egypt
Precipitation (POWER)

        

        

          
Not yet significant (p = 0.74, NASA POWER 1981-2025). Residual skewness 1.95,
             normality p < 0.001. The analytical and Weatherhead formulas assume Gaussian
             noise and return crossing years around 2123-2128. The Monte Carlo estimate
             returns 2051 (85% PI 2036-2068). The methods diverge by 77 years. Tidemark
             detects the divergence, diagnoses the cause, and directs you to the correct
             estimate. This is the central demonstration of the four-method cross-validation.
